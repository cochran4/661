Structural equation models tell a story

We begin construction by telling a story about how the world generated our data. The story proceeds in an order. First, the world gives us one variable. Then it gives us another, possibly using variables it has already generated. A structural equation model makes this sequence explicit.

The world gives us \(X\), then \(Y\)

Start with two random variables, \(X\) and \(Y\). The world first generates \(X\). For now, we do not care how. Once \(X\) has taken a value, the world generates \(Y\) using a probability model that can depend on \(X\):

\[ X\sim P_X, \qquad Y\mid X=x\sim P_{Y\mid X=x}. \]

This notation gives us a recipe for sampling one observation:

  1. Draw a value \(x\) from the distribution of \(X\).
  2. Having observed \(X=x\), draw a value \(y\) from the conditional distribution of \(Y\) given \(X=x\).

The sequence matters. The rule that generates \(Y\) is allowed to use \(X\) because \(X\) has already been generated. In this story, the rule that generates \(X\) cannot use \(Y\), because \(Y\) does not yet exist.

Together, these two steps specify a joint probability model. We can express the same idea through the factorization

\[ P(X=x,Y=y)=P(X=x)P(Y=y\mid X=x) \]

when \(X\) and \(Y\) are discrete. The sequential story and the joint distribution are two descriptions of the same probability model.

Suppose \(X\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(0.4)\) and \(Y\mid X=x\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(0.1+0.6x)\). The world has generated \(X=1\). What happens next?

The world draws \(Y\) from a \(\operatorname{Bernoulli}(0.7)\) distribution, because

\[ 0.1+0.6(1)=0.7. \]

Thus, the next step produces \(Y=1\) with probability \(0.7\) and \(Y=0\) with probability \(0.3\).

Using the same model, what changes if the world instead generates \(X=0\)?

The sequence of steps does not change, but the distribution used in the second step does. The world draws \(Y\) from a \(\operatorname{Bernoulli}(0.1)\) distribution.

Suppose \(X\sim\operatorname{Normal}(0,1)\) and \(Y\mid X=x\sim\operatorname{Normal}(2+x,4)\). If the first step produces \(X=-1\), what distribution is used in the second step?

The world draws \(Y\) from

\[ \operatorname{Normal}(2+(-1),4)=\operatorname{Normal}(1,4). \]

These exercises may look simple, but the habit is essential: stop at a particular point in the data-generating sequence, identify what has already happened, and use that information to determine the next step.

Regression models describe the next step

Case study: Right heart catheterization

Between 1989 and 1994, the Study to Understand Prognoses and Preferences for Outcomes and Risks of Treatments (SUPPORT) followed critically ill adults receiving care in five teaching hospitals in the United States. In 1996, Connors and colleagues used data from 5,735 of these patients to study right heart catheterization (RHC), a procedure used to measure cardiac function. They asked whether receiving an RHC during the first 24 hours of intensive care was associated with survival, length of stay, intensity of care, and cost (Connors et al. 1996).

The data are available through the Vanderbilt University Department of Biostatistics dataset repository. In the row labeled Right heart catheterization dataset, the CSV file is linked under the ASCII column. The repository also provides a description of the study and dataset, a direct link to the CSV file, and documentation for the variables and their coding. One row in the dataset represents one critically ill patient. We will introduce the variables as we need them.

A regression function describes the conditional mean used in the second step:

\[ \mu(x):=E[Y\mid X=x]. \]

If we also observe a variable \(A\) before \(Y\), we might instead propose a function \(\mu(X,A;\theta)\), specified by unknown parameters \(\theta\), such that

\[ E[Y\mid X,A]=\mu(X,A;\theta). \]

Data can be used to estimate \(\theta\), producing \(\widehat\theta\) and an estimated conditional mean \(\mu(X,A;\widehat\theta)\).

A conditional-mean model does not generally specify the entire conditional distribution of \(Y\). To sample \(Y\), the world also needs to know how values vary around that mean. For a binary \(Y\), however, its conditional mean is its conditional probability of equaling 1 and therefore determines its conditional Bernoulli distribution.

The art of regression includes deciding:

  • what form \(\mu\) should take; and
  • how to estimate \(\theta\).

Linear regression

In linear regression, we choose a form for \(\mu(X,A;\theta)\) that is linear in its parameters \(\theta\). For example, we might choose

\[ \mu(X,A;\theta):=\theta^{\mathsf T} \begin{bmatrix} 1\\X\\A \end{bmatrix}, \]

or include an interaction:

\[ \mu(X,A;\theta):=\theta^{\mathsf T} \begin{bmatrix} 1\\X\\A\\XA \end{bmatrix}. \]

Ordinary least squares estimates \(\theta\) by minimizing squared error.

Logistic regression

When \(Y\) is binary, \(E[Y\mid X,A]\) lies in \([0,1]\). Logistic regression respects this restriction by modeling the logit of the conditional mean as a linear function. The logit function is

\[ \operatorname{logit}(p):=\log\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right), \]

and its inverse is

\[ \operatorname{logit}^{-1}(u):=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-u)}. \]

For example, we might specify

\[ E[Y\mid X,A] =\operatorname{logit}^{-1} \left( \theta^{\mathsf T} \begin{bmatrix} 1\\X\\A \end{bmatrix} \right). \]

Once \(X\) and \(A\) have been generated, this model supplies the probability used to generate the binary variable \(Y\).

Using fitted response models

Once we have \(\widehat\theta\), we can evaluate \(\mu(X,a;\widehat\theta)\) for each person in the dataset and compute its empirical expectation:

\[ E_N[\mu(X,a;\widehat\theta)]. \]

From a sequence to structural assignments

We now make the mechanisms behind the sequence explicit. Introduce variables \(U_X\) and \(U_Y\) that contain whatever additional inputs the world uses when generating \(X\) and \(Y\). We write

\[ X:=f_X(U_X), \qquad Y:=f_Y(X,U_Y). \]

These are assignments, not ordinary algebraic equations. Read them from left to right:

  1. Generate \(U_X\), then assign \(X\) the value \(f_X(U_X)\).
  2. Generate \(U_Y\), then assign \(Y\) the value \(f_Y(X,U_Y)\) using the value of \(X\) produced in the first step.

The second assignment records the direction in the story: \(X\) can help determine \(Y\). It does not say that we should rearrange the expression and use \(Y\) to generate \(X\).

For the binary example above, let \(U_X\) and \(U_Y\) be independent uniform random variables on \((0,1)\) and define

\[ X:=1_{\{U_X\leq0.4\}}, \qquad Y:=1_{\{U_Y\leq0.1+0.6X\}}. \]

These assignments reproduce the earlier sequential model: \(X\) equals 1 with probability \(0.4\), and after \(X\) is generated, the probability that \(Y=1\) is \(0.1+0.6X\).

Follow the assignments when \(U_X=0.3\) and \(U_Y=0.5\). What values do \(X\) and \(Y\) take?

Because \(0.3\leq0.4\), the first assignment gives \(X=1\). The second threshold is therefore \(0.1+0.6(1)=0.7\). Because \(0.5\leq0.7\), the second assignment gives \(Y=1\).

Keep \(U_X=0.3\) but change \(U_Y\) to \(0.8\). What changes?

The first assignment still gives \(X=1\). The threshold for \(Y\) remains \(0.7\), but now \(0.8>0.7\), so the second assignment gives \(Y=0\).

This simple model establishes the basic skill we will use throughout Construction: read a data-generating story in sequence and determine what each assignment produces from the information available at that point.

In the next section, we will specify this construction more generally to obtain a nonparametric structural equation model (NPSEM). We will then use the structure of those assignments to draw causal graphs.
